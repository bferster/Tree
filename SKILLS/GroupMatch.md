The core idea: optimal assignment + group normalization
Treat it as a two-layer problem:
Layer 1 — individual pairwise similarity. For every person i in the 1870 family and every entry j in the 1860 schedule holding, compute a similarity sim(i, j) from the only attributes both sides share:
Age progression: expected 1860 age = 1870 age − 10. Score the deviation with a Gaussian (or a discrete cascade like your Lever B: exact > ±1 > ±2 > ±3 > ±5), because slave schedule ages are notoriously rounded and estimated. A wider sigma than your census-to-census matcher is appropriate here — maybe σ ≈ 3–4 years instead of 2.
Gender: hard gate. Mismatch → sim = 0.
Race: soft signal. B/M vs. B/M agreement is weak-positive; B vs. W is a knockout, consistent with your existing scoring rules. Note that mulatto/black coding is unstable across enumerators, so treat B↔M as neutral-to-positive, never a penalty.
Layer 2 — optimal one-to-one matching. Run the Hungarian algorithm (or greedy with conflict resolution, like your Phase 3) on the sim matrix to find the assignment that maximizes total similarity, with a minimum threshold below which a pairing counts as "unmatched" rather than a bad match. This gives you N matched pairs out of M (1870 family size) and M′ (1860 holding size).
Layer 3 — group score with a smart denominator. The group-linking formula from Fu et al., which appears in your graph-matching reference, is exactly this:
score = Σ sim(matched pairs) / (M + M′ − N)
The denominator penalizes unmatched people on either side. But — and this is where your intuition about missing/added members comes in — you should excuse the expected absences before penalizing:
A 1870 family member with a birth year after 1860 cannot appear in the schedule. Remove them from M entirely. They're not evidence against the match; they're not evidence at all.
Conversely, unmatched 1860 entries are weaker negative evidence than unmatched 1870 adults, because death, sale, and family separation between 1860 and 1865 were common. You can down-weight them (count each as, say, 0.5 in the denominator) rather than fully penalizing.
An unmatched 1870 adult who should have been alive and enslaved in 1860 is the strongest negative signal — full penalty.
This "excused vs. unexcused absence" asymmetry is what separates a naive set-overlap score from one that models the actual historical process.
Layer 4 — structural (edge) evidence
The graph-matching paper's key finding is that pairwise attribute matching alone plateaus around F ≈ 0.75, while adding household structure pushes it above 0.93. You can't use role pairs (the schedule has no relationships), but you can use age-gap structure: the pattern of age differences between members is preserved across ten years even when absolute ages are misreported. A woman 24 years older than one child and 22 years older than another in 1870 should show the same ~24/~22 spacing in 1860. Score the similarity of the age-difference matrix between matched members — this catches cases where individual ages are all off by the same enumerator error, and it also catches false matches where individual ages coincidentally fit but the internal spacing doesn't. The joint age–sex profile of a whole group rarely coincides by chance; the spacing structure is even rarer.
Layer 5 — the surname/enslaver bridge
Since the 1870 side has names and the 1860 side has the enslaver's name, fold in your existing wall-pierce signal: phonetic (NYSIIS/Metaphone) match between the 1870 family surname and the enslaver surname, weighted by surname rarity in the county pool. Also geography — same county/district is nearly a precondition. Freedmen's Bureau records, where they exist, override the probabilistic score entirely, as your plan already notes.
Turning the score into a probability
A raw score isn't a probability, and this is where most household-linkage designs cheat. Two honest routes:
Calibration (the pragmatic route). Build a small labeled set — your case studies (Henderson Goings, Aggie Howell, Benjamin Downey) plus Freedmen's Bureau–confirmed links are gold labels — and fit a logistic regression mapping (group score, structural score, surname signal, group size) → P(match). This mirrors what the graph-matching paper did, and group size must be a feature: a perfect 8-person profile match is vastly stronger evidence than a perfect 2-person match.
Chance correction (the principled complement). For a given 1870 family, run the same scoring against everycandidate holding in the blocking pool (same county, plausible enslaver surnames). The probability isn't just "how well does this holding fit" but "how much better does it fit than the alternatives." A softmax over candidate scores — or an empirical p-value from the score distribution across non-candidates — converts fit into distinctiveness. A holding of six people with generic age/sex spread will match many families decently; the score should reflect that.
In Fellegi–Sunter terms, layer 1–4 gives you the m-side (agreement given a true match), and the pool comparison gives you the u-side (agreement by chance). The ratio, calibrated, is your 0.0–1.0.
Sequence alignment (the sleeper candidate)
Both of your rosters have a canonical order. Census families are enumerated head-first, then spouse, then children by descending age; slave schedules typically list entries by descending age within a holding. That means a family and a holding aren't just sets — they're sequences, and comparing them becomes an alignment problem, solvable with Needleman–Wunsch-style dynamic programming exactly as in bioinformatics. Matches, insertions (children born after 1860), and deletions (deaths, sales) fall out of the alignment naturally, and — this is the elegant part — your excusal weights are the gap penalties: a gap opposite a post-1860 child costs 0, a gap opposite an unexcused adult costs 1.0, position-dependent gap costs encode the persistence table. The DP recurrence finds the optimal alignment in O(M×M′) without any assignment solver, and it adds one signal the Hungarian approach throws away: order preservation. A matching that requires crossing the age ordering (the 1870 40-year-old pairing with the schedule's youngest entry while the 1870 child pairs with the oldest) is almost certainly wrong, and alignment penalizes it structurally while unordered assignment doesn't notice. Since your GroupMatcher's _assign is deliberately swappable, an alignment-based assigner is a cheap experiment — same inputs, same output shape, and you can compare the two on your case studies directly.
